Claim 1 Navier–Stokes
ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + μ∇²v + ρg
ρ density v velocity field p pressure μ dynamic viscosity g gravity vector
Plain English Decoder
The Navier–Stokes equations describe how the velocity of a fluid evolves under the influence of pressure gradients, internal friction (viscosity), and external body forces like gravity. They are the foundation of all continuum fluid mechanics.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
These equations govern every aerodynamic phenomenon — from the thin boundary layer over a wing to the turbulent wake behind a fuselage. Solving them (even approximately) is the central challenge of computational fluid dynamics.

Claim 2 Prandtl Induced Drag
CDi = CL² / (π · AR · e)
CDi induced drag coeff. CL lift coefficient AR aspect ratio e span efficiency factor
Plain English Decoder
The induced aerodynamic drag penalty grows with the square of the lift coefficient, constrained by the geometric aspect ratio and the spanwise efficiency factor. Higher lift means more drag — and the penalty is worse for short, stubby wings.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
The Wright brothers discovered that roll control cannot exist independently of yaw control. Twisting one wing upward increases CL, which by this exact equation multiplies CDi, pulling the machine into a spin unless the vertical rudder is deflected simultaneously.

Claim 3 Canard Pitch Equilibrium
Cm = Cm0 + ((xcg − xac) / c) · CL − Vcanard · CL,canard(δe)
Cm pitching moment coeff. Cm0 zero‑lift camber moment xcg−xac static margin c mean aerodynamic chord Vcanard canard volume ratio
Plain English Decoder
Net pitching moment coefficient balances the zero‑lift wing camber moment, the center‑of‑gravity static margin across the mean aerodynamic chord, total wing lift, and the forward canard elevator control lift scaled by the canard volume ratio.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
The Wrights placed the horizontal elevator in front (a canard) rather than behind the wings. When pulling up to climb, a canard creates positive upward lift rather than the downward force produced by a conventional tail. This made the Flyer inherently more stable in pitch.

Claim 4 Bernoulli's Principle
p + ½ρv² + ρgh = const.
p static pressure ρ density v flow speed g gravity h elevation
Plain English Decoder
Along a streamline, the total mechanical energy per unit volume of a fluid is conserved. Where speed increases, pressure decreases — and vice versa. This is the fundamental principle behind lift generation.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
The pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces of a wing is what creates lift. By accelerating air over the curved top surface, Bernoulli tells us the pressure drops — and the wing is sucked upward into the region of lower pressure.

Claim 5 Reynolds Number
Re = ρvL / μ
Re Reynolds number ρ density v flow speed L characteristic length μ dynamic viscosity
Plain English Decoder
The Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces in a flow. It tells us whether the flow will be smooth (laminar) or chaotic (turbulent) — a critical predictor for drag and heat transfer.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
At low Re (creeping flow), viscosity dominates and flow is laminar. At high Re (like full‑scale aircraft), inertia dominates and flow becomes turbulent — which actually delays separation and can reduce overall drag on a well‑designed airfoil.

Claim 6 Lift Equation
L = ½ρv² · S · CL
L lift force ρ air density v airspeed S wing planform area CL lift coefficient
Plain English Decoder
Lift is proportional to the dynamic pressure (½ρv²), the wing area, and the lift coefficient. To get more lift, you can fly faster, increase wing area, or increase the angle of attack — but each comes with trade‑offs in drag and structural load.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
This simple equation is the starting point for every aircraft design. For a given weight, the required CL determines the wing loading and angle of attack. The Wright Flyer's low speed meant it needed a large wing area and a high‑lift airfoil to get airborne.

Claim 7 Continuity Equation
ρ1A1v1 = ρ2A2v2
ρ density A cross‑sectional area v flow speed
Plain English Decoder
For a steady flow, the mass flow rate is conserved: what goes in must come out. If the flow area decreases, the speed must increase (and vice versa) — provided density is constant.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
This is why air accelerates over the top of a wing (area constriction) and why a venturi tube can be used to measure flow speed. In supersonic flow, the density changes too — which is why the area rule becomes critical for high‑speed aircraft.

Claim 8 Euler Equations (Inviscid)
ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + ρg
ρ density v velocity field t time p pressure g gravity
Plain English Decoder
The Euler equations are the inviscid (friction‑free) limit of the Navier–Stokes equations. They describe how an ideal fluid moves under pressure and gravity, but with no viscosity — a good approximation for high‑Reynolds number flows away from solid boundaries.
Physical Principle & Engineering Insight
These equations form the basis of potential flow theory and are used to compute lift distributions on wings using panel methods. They also explain why a turning vane or a curved surface can generate a pressure difference — the foundation of airfoil design.
